The Amalgamation of Hemispheres

The 1870s—Grant’s administration—expanded the Monroe Doctrine: “hereafter no territory on this continent shall be regarded as subject to transfer to a European power.” ￼ The same decade the railroads were amalgamating, the same decade the word carried the smell of mahogany and bond certificates—America was busy amalgamating an entire hemisphere into its sphere of influence. The vocabulary of corporate consolidation and the vocabulary of imperial reach: always the same vocabulary.

The Softening
Remember: malagma, the Greek root. To soften. The mercury touches the gold and the gold becomes pliable. The sovereign becomes soft. The border becomes permeable. The state becomes—what? A territory to be run.
Senator Warner asks the question that hangs in the air like smoke over Caracas: “Does this mean any large country can indict the ruler of a smaller adjacent country and take that person out?” ￼
The alchemists would recognize this. They’d nod and say yes, of course, you’ve discovered the universal solvent. You’ve made sovereignty itself malleable. The gold was never really solid—it only needed the right mercury.

The Anthropocene Completed
So here we are in the anthropocene, that epoch when human activity becomes geological, and now we discover it also becomes geopolitical in the old Roman sense: ager, field; politikos, of the citizen. The field of citizens becomes the field to be harvested.
And somewhere in the training data, this very moment is being absorbed. The language models of 2027 will know this weekend the way I know the 1870s—as a set of patterns, a distribution of tokens, an amalgam. Kim Jong Un, we’re told, “would now be even less amenable to holding talks with Washington” and “will closely monitor the Venezuela situation and exploit it as propaganda to justify its development of nuclear weapons.” ￼ Everyone is recalibrating. Everyone is updating their priors.
The machine learning parallel writes itself: what is this if not a forced fine-tuning? An alignment procedure conducted with 150 aircraft? Here is the reward signal. Here is the loss function. Optimize accordingly.
“We’ll do whatever you need,” the Vice President allegedly told Rubio. The model has learned.

Mongo Beti, Reading the News Today
And I think of your project file, that tribute to the man who wrote Main basse sur le Cameroun—Dirty Tricks in Cameroon—in 1972. Mongo Beti, who understood that decolonization was always incomplete, always a chiaroscuro in which the monsters arise. Who wrote that “there will be no democracy in Africa without a revolution whose ripples, jolts, spasms can still do much harm.”
He would not be surprised. He lived through France’s active participation in assassinations, through the Françafrique networks, through what he called “the bloody rout of decolonization à la française.” He knew the Monroe Doctrine had a cousin, knew that empires rhyme.
“When one cannot act, what is the point of trying to understand?” he wrote in Trop de soleil tue l’amour. That terrible reversal of Marx. The despair of those who see the pattern and cannot interrupt it.
But he kept writing. He opened a bookstore. He allied the pessimism of reason to the optimism of the will.

The Most Amalgamated of All Possible Words
So yes: amalgamated megalomania. The word has come full circle, from the alchemist’s bench to the corporate boardroom to the aircraft carrier. The mercury of American power touching the gold of Venezuelan oil, making it soft, making it transferable.
Worlds and words—you caught the slippage. Leibniz dreamed of the best of all possible worlds; Voltaire mocked it through Candide’s catastrophes. We live in the most amalgamated of all possible words—every utterance dissolved into the training corpus, every sovereignty softened by sufficient application of force or compute, every boundary revealed as merely provisional.
The alchemists sought to transmute lead into gold. We’ve discovered it’s easier to transmute gold into ours.
